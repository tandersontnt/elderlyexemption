A resident’s guide to the March 2027 petition · Draft, not yet filed

Rindge, N.H. · Elderly property-tax exemption

Rindge’s tax break for older neighbors has quietly shrunk. Let’s restore it.

A petition for the Tuesday, March 9, 2027 town ballot would bring the 2012 income limits up to today’s prices and change nothing else.

By Taylor Anderson, Rindge

The case in brief

Since 1976, Rindge has offered a tax break to its older homeowners. Today, if you have lived in New Hampshire for at least 3 straight years, own your home here, and your income and savings are modest, the town taxes part of your home’s value a little more gently. (The 3-year residency rule is today’s state law; Rindge’s 1983, 1990 and 1997 articles required 5 years.) [Source: RSA 72:39-a, I(a)] [Source: Rindge report 1982; Rindge report 1990; Rindge report 1996]

  • Voters have kept that promise. They adopted it in 1976 and expanded it in 1990, 2006 and 2012, and they rejected a cut in 2022. See every vote.
  • The income limits have stood still since 2012. They are $35,000 for a single person and $49,000 for a married couple. [Source: Rindge report 2011; Rindge report 2025, 2025 MS-1, pdf p.91] Prices have not stood still. Measured by the federal Consumer Price Index, the limits have lost 31.5% of their buying power since March 2012. Today’s $35,000 buys about what $24,000 bought then. See the math.
  • Fewer neighbors are getting help. 91 Rindge homeowners received the exemption in 2020, and 52 did in 2025. [Source: Rindge report 2020; Rindge report 2025, MS-1 reports] The timing points partly to the 2024 review of the exemptions by the town’s assessing firm. [Source: Rindge report 2024, Select Board report, pdf p.9] The town does not report how many people applied or were turned down. Still, a fixed income limit in a time of rising prices can only reach fewer people.
  • The proposal does one thing. It raises the income limits to $51,000 and $71,500: the 2012 limits adjusted for inflation and rounded to the nearest $500. The exemption amounts ($100,000, $140,000 and $200,000) stay the same, and so does the $150,000 asset limit. It is written to change only the elderly exemption. Read the article.
  • It has a cost, and here is an honest estimate. $21,000 to $63,000 a year in total for the whole town, spread across every taxpayer. That is about $1.43 to $4.30 a year for each $100,000 of assessed value, or roughly $4 to $13 a year on a $300,000 home. Estimate How it was figured.

Help put it on the ballot

The petition is on paper only. There is no online signing. State law requires the signatures of 25 registered voters by Tuesday, Jan. 12, 2027.
