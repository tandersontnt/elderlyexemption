Inflation math

How $35,000 becomes $51,000

In brief

The proposal does not pick new numbers. It takes the limits voters set in March 2012 and gives them back the same buying power, using the federal Consumer Price Index.

  • 31.5%Buying power lost by the income limits since March 2012
  • 40.4%Buying power lost since March 2006 by the $150,000 asset limit
  • 46.03%Price rise, March 2012 to August 2026

The price index

We use the Consumer Price Index for All Urban Consumers (CPI-U), U.S. city average, all items, not seasonally adjusted. This is the standard cost-of-living index published monthly by the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics. We use March 2012 because voters set today’s limits that month. August 2026 is the latest month that had been published when these figures were checked. [Source: BLS CPI-U]

Rounding takes the exact figures down slightly, so the proposed limits are a little below full 2012 buying power, not above it.

What the current limits have lost

Buying power lost since the limits were set
StepCalculationResult
Share of buying power lost1 − (229.392 ÷ 334.980)31.5%
Today’s single limit in 2012 dollars$35,000 × 229.392 ÷ 334.980$23,968
Today’s married limit in 2012 dollars$49,000 (pending fact check) × 229.392 ÷ 334.980$33,555

In plain terms, a single person at today’s $35,000 limit can buy about what $23,968, or about $24,000, bought when voters set it.

The $150,000 asset limit, set in March 2006, has lost 40.4% by the same measure: 1 − (199.800 ÷ 334.980). The exemption amounts are a different case. They are slices of assessed value, not consumer prices, and the 2025 revaluation nearly doubled assessments: the town’s net valuation went from $766,781,321 in 2024 to $1,466,136,314 in 2025. So the exemption amounts now cover a much smaller share of a home’s value. [Source: Rindge report 2024; Rindge report 2025] The proposal does not change them.

Want to check the arithmetic? The full monthly CPI-U series is in cpi-u-cpiaucns.csv, and every figure on this page is in data.csv.
